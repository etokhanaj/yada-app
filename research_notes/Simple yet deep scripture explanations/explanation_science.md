# Explanation science: making explanations easy to understand and still deep (adults, phone screens)

## 1. Cognitive load, worked examples, signalling and segmenting: what Mayer's research says about short text explanations on a phone

### Takeaway
The strongest and most consistent multimedia principles that carry over to text-only phone reading are coherence (cut extraneous and "seductive" material), signalling (make the structure of the essential ideas explicit) and segmenting (learner-paced chunks). Each shows medium-to-large effects in Mayer's own tallies. The newest independent meta-analysis finds a smaller overall effect (g = 0.37), with effect sizes falling over time and varying by moderator, so the single-study numbers are upper bounds.

### Cited Findings
- Mayer's own tallies of his experiments: coherence principle (exclude extraneous material), median d = 0.86, supported in 18 of 19 tests. Signalling (cues that highlight how the essential material is organised), d = 0.69, 15 of 16 tests. Segmenting (learner-paced segments, not one continuous unit), d = 0.67, 7 of 7 tests — [The Future of Multimedia Learning, EdTech Books](https://edtechbooks.org/docx/10349)
- Independent meta-analysis of Mayer's corpus: 92 articles, 181 studies, 591 effects, overall Hedges g = 0.37 (multilevel model). The largest significant effects were for removing seductive detail, modality, personalisation, the multimedia principle, sentence-level coherence and self-explanation. Effects were moderated by principle, media type, age, domain, country, treatment duration, type of dependent variable, year (a small decline per year) and authorship order — Cromley & Chen (2025), *Educational Research Review*, [NSF PAR listing](https://par.nsf.gov/search/author:%22Cromley,%20Jennifer%20G%22)
- Seductive details meta-analysis: 58 studies, 68 effects, g = -0.33 (interesting but irrelevant material hurts learning). The harm was larger when the details were static or placed at the end of the material (g = -0.70) — Sundararajan & Adesope (2020), summarised by [ACU Inspire](https://inspire.acu.edu.au/articles/seductive-details) and [WSU News](https://news.wsu.edu/2020/03/19/seductive-details-inhibit-learning/)
- Reading on mobile: across NN/g studies (1,629 reading instances), there were "no practical differences" in comprehension between phone and computer. Easy articles were about 400 words at US grade 8; hard ones about 990 words at grade 12. Phone readers were slower on hard passages, about 30 ms more per word, which suggests they kept comprehension up by working harder or re-reading. NN/g still recommends brevity and prioritisation for complex content on mobile, and testing complex material on phones — [NN/g, Reading Content on Mobile Devices](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-content/)
- A 2010/2011 NN/g study had found lower comprehension on mobile. The 2016 reversal is attributed to better screens and users' experience with phones — [NN/g 2011](https://nngroup.com/articles/mobile-content-is-twice-as-difficult-2011); [KU CTE summary](https://cte.ku.edu/reason-reconsider-students-mobile-reading)

### Inferences
- For a phone explanation, the levers with the best evidence are: (a) remove anything that is interesting but not needed for the core point (coherence and seductive details); (b) signal structure with a clear opening claim, explicit connectives such as "because", "so" and "but", and headings or ordering that mirror the logic; (c) segment into reader-paced units, so the reader taps to continue and does not get one long scroll.
- "Interesting asides" such as trivia, colourful historical anecdotes and tangential word facts are the classic seductive detail. If they are kept, they belong in a separate layer that the reader opens, not inline. The g = -0.70 for end-placed details suggests a "fun fact" tacked on at the end is risky.
- Phone screens do not lower comprehension of hard text, but they do raise its cost, so cutting length matters more as difficulty rises.

### Gaps
- Most of Mayer's experiments use narrated animations or text plus diagrams on desktop or paper, with students. I found no RCT that tests signalling or segmenting specifically on short text-only explanations on phones.
- The per-principle effect sizes in the Cromley & Chen meta-analysis were not available in the abstract I could access, so only the ranking is reported here.

## 2. Progressive disclosure and layered explanation ("ELI5, then 15, then expert"): help or harm? Illusion of explanatory depth and expertise reversal

### Takeaway
Layering is supported indirectly. The expertise reversal effect and the text-coherence × prior-knowledge interaction show that one fixed level of support cannot serve novices and knowledgeable readers equally, which argues for letting readers choose depth. The same research warns that a maximally smooth, simplified layer can produce a feeling of understanding without the understanding itself, and that very explicit text can rob knowledgeable readers of deep processing. UX evidence favours at most two disclosure levels.

### Cited Findings
- Expertise reversal effect: formats that help low-knowledge learners are often ineffective, or even harmful, for high-knowledge learners. Strong guidance that reduces working-memory load for novices can impose redundant load on experts, who may learn better with minimal guidance — [Kalyuga (2007), Educational Psychology Review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2Fs10648-007-9054-3); [PDF](https://www.uky.edu/~gmswan3/EDC608/Kalyuga2007_Article_ExpertiseReversalEffectAndItsI.pdf)
- A review of expertise reversal across domains (Kalyuga, Rikers & Paas, 2012) identifies common mechanisms. I found no pooled meta-analytic effect size — [UNSW listing](https://unsworks.unsw.edu.au/entities/publication/c26cbc05-47ae-4c02-be87-97ab0e0dca70/full)
- Text coherence × prior knowledge: all readers recalled more from a fully coherent, explicit text, but high-knowledge readers did better on situation-model (deep, inferential) tests with a less explicit text that forced gap-filling inferences. Low-knowledge readers did better with the explicit version — McNamara, Kintsch, Songer & Kintsch (1996), "Are good texts always better?", summarised by [BYU Editing Research](https://editingresearch.byu.edu/2022/11/23/how-less-clarity-could-improve-active-processing) and [Utrecht](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/coherence-marking-prior-knowledge-and-comprehension-of-informativ/)
- Illusion of explanatory depth (IOED): people rate their understanding of causal systems highly, then rate it much lower after trying to write a step-by-step explanation. The illusion is stronger for explanatory knowledge than for facts, procedures or narratives — Rozenblit & Keil (2002), [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illusion_of_explanatory_depth); [original PDF](https://repositories.cdlib.org/content/qt582378mf/qt582378mf.pdf?t=op2kua)
- Asking people to generate a mechanistic explanation, but not to list reasons, reduced their IOED and moderated their political attitudes — Fernbach, Rogers, Fox & Sloman (2013), *Psychological Science* — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23620547/)
- Progressive disclosure: show the most important things first and more on request. This improves learnability, efficiency and error rates. Designs with more than two disclosure levels typically have poor usability because users get lost. The way to the next layer needs strong "information scent" — [NN/g, Progressive Disclosure](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/)

### Inferences
- A layered design such as "short answer → deeper explanation" fits expertise reversal: novices get high-coherence support and knowledgeable readers can skip it. NN/g's guidance suggests two layers (plus perhaps a reference layer), not three or four age-graded rewrites.
- Risk: a polished top layer can create an IOED, where the reader feels they understand. Mitigations consistent with the evidence: make the top layer causal or mechanistic (the "why", not just the "what"), state what is still open, and invite a one-line self-explanation or retell.
- Keep the layers consistent. The deeper layer should add mechanism, evidence and nuance to the same claim, not restate it in harder words. A deeper layer that contradicts the simple layer would undermine trust (see section 4).
- The McNamara result suggests the expert layer can leave more for the reader to infer. The novice layer should spell out the connections between ideas.

### Gaps
- I found no RCT that directly compares "ELI5 → ELI15 → expert" graded rewrites against a single well-written text for adult comprehension or transfer.
- I found no meta-analytic effect size for expertise reversal or for progressive disclosure in reading contexts. NN/g's guidance comes from usability practice, not controlled trials.

## 3. Plain-language standards (plainlanguage.gov, GOV.UK, ISO 24495-1:2023): reading age, sentence length, jargon; readability formulas and their limits

### Takeaway
The standards agree on outcomes (readers can find, understand and use the information) and on rules of thumb: average sentence length of about 15–20 words, a 20–25-word ceiling, everyday words, and technical terms defined on first use. GOV.UK targets a reading age of 9, and specialists also prefer plain English. Readability formulas measure only word and sentence length and correlate poorly with real comprehension, so they are a rough flag, not a target.

### Cited Findings
- ISO 24495-1:2023 has four governing principles: readers get what they need (relevant), can easily find it (findable), easily understand it (understandable) and easily use it (usable). It applies to text-based print and digital documents. It was drafted by experts from 25 countries in 19 languages — [IPLF on the ISO standard](https://www.iplfederation.org/the-iso-plain-language-standard-part1-and-part2); [EU AccessibleEU](https://accessible-eu-centre.ec.europa.eu/content-corner/digital-library/iso-24495-12023-plain-language-part-1-governing-principles-and-guidelines_en)
- GOV.UK targets a reading age of 9, based on the average UK reading age — [Somerset Council playbook, citing GDS](https://playbook.somerset.gov.uk/digital-inclusion-and-accessibility/creating-accessible-documents/document-language-and-reading-age); [Writing for GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/content-design/writing-for-gov-uk) (now redirects to the publishing guidance site; the original page could not be retrieved directly)
- Higher-literacy and specialist readers also prefer plain English because it lets them understand faster. In research on legal documents, 80% of people preferred sentences in clear English, and the preference was stronger for complex issues — [States of Jersey service manual, adapting GOV.UK guidance](https://www.gov.je/ServiceManual/BrandDesign/Pages/TechnicalSpecialistContent.aspx)
- Sentence length: plain-language guidance recommends no more than 20 words per sentence, with some guides saying under 25 and an average of about 15. The Plain English Campaign advises an average of 15–20 words — [Federal Plain Language Guidelines (KY mirror)](https://revenue.ky.gov/DOR%20Training%20Materials/Federal%20Plain%20Language%20Guidelines.pdf); [Storm ID](https://stormid.com/blog/content-accessibility-plain-english/)
- Jargon: use common words instead of technical or internal terms where possible. When a technical term is necessary, define it, and spell out abbreviations on first use — [Federal Plain Language Guidelines](https://revenue.ky.gov/DOR%20Training%20Materials/Federal%20Plain%20Language%20Guidelines.pdf); [Iowa DX training](https://dxtraining.iowa.gov/use-plain-language)
- Limits of readability formulas (Redish): most of what makes a document usable is not in the formulas. They assume short words are always better, penalise bulleted lists, and ignore content choice, organisation and headings. Improving a readability score does not usually improve comprehension — [Government of Canada, Our Languages blog](https://www.noslangues-ourlanguages.gc.ca/en/blogue-blog/readability-formulas-eng); [Effortmark (Caroline Jarrett)](https://effortmark.co.uk/?p=3586)
- Theoretical critique: readability formulas ignore much of current knowledge about the reading process and rest on shaky statistics — [Illinois IDEALS technical report](https://www.ideals.illinois.edu/items/15551/bitstreams/54962/data.pdf)

### Inferences
- Use "reading age 9 / grade 6–8" as a default for the top layer. Keep sentences averaging 15–20 words, with none over about 25.
- Technical terms (in Yada's case, original-language words, grammatical terms and theological terms) can stay if they are defined in plain words the first time they appear and used consistently afterwards. This keeps depth without jargon walls.
- Use readability scores only as a lint check in the pipeline. Validate with readers (section 5), because formulas cannot see coherence, structure or whether the content is the right content.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve the current GOV.UK guidance page directly (redirect/404), so its exact wording on reading age and the 25-word guidance is sourced via secondary pages.
- The full text of ISO 24495-1 is paywalled. Its guidelines on sentence length and terminology could not be quoted, and I am not aware that it sets a numeric reading-age target.
- I could not confirm the original study behind the "80% preferred clear English" figure (it is commonly attributed to Kimble's research on legal writing).

## 4. Keeping nuance and uncertainty in simple writing: hedging, "scholars disagree", confidence language and calibrated trust

### Takeaway
Stating uncertainty plainly, as a range or a clear verbal statement, lowers confidence in the specific claim, as it should, without significantly lowering trust in the source. The exception is "consensus uncertainty" (experts disagree), which is the framing most often associated with reduced credibility and has never been found to have positive effects. So "scholars disagree" needs care: say what they disagree about, why, and what is agreed.

### Cited Findings
- Van der Bles, van der Linden, Freeman & Spiegelhalter (2020, *PNAS*): five UK experiments, N = 5,780. Numbers were shown with no uncertainty, with a numeric range, or with a verbal statement of uncertainty. Expressing uncertainty numerically reduced confidence in the number but did not cause people to lose trust in the source. Reactions to uncertainty did not differ by political ideology — [Univ. of Groningen record](https://research.rug.nl/en/publications/the-effects-of-communicating-uncertainty-on-public-trust-in-facts/); [AI Impacts summary](https://blog.aiimpacts.org/p/paper-summary-the-effects-of-communicating)
- Gustafson & Rice (2020, *Public Understanding of Science*), a review of k = 48 experiments across four uncertainty types (deficient, technical, scientific, consensus). Overall effects were mixed. Most negative effects on credibility and belief came from consensus uncertainty (disagreement or conflict among scientists), which was never found to have positive effects — [Rice faculty page](https://rrice.faculty.comm.ucsb.edu/a149.htm); [PDF](https://rrice.faculty.comm.ucsb.edu/A149GustafsonRice2020ReviewEffectsUncertaintyPublicScienceCommunication.pdf)

### Inferences
- Use plain, specific uncertainty statements. For example: "The Greek can mean either X or Y. Most translations choose X because …". This states the uncertainty and its basis, which the van der Bles results suggest keeps trust in the source.
- Avoid bare "scholars disagree" or "this is debated". This is the consensus-uncertainty framing linked to lower credibility. Better: name the positions, say what each rests on, say what is agreed, and say what is uncertain.
- Hedge once, at the claim level, not on every sentence. Scattered hedges add words and blur which claim is uncertain. This is an inference from plain-language economy, not a tested finding.
- Keep confidence language consistent across layers, so the simple layer does not sound certain while the deep layer reveals doubt.

### Gaps
- Most uncertainty research concerns numbers (statistics, estimates). I found no experiments on verbal confidence labels for interpretive or textual claims (for example, contested readings of a text).
- I found no study that tests verbal confidence scales (for example, "likely" or "possible") for calibration in a plain-language reading context. Verbal probability terms are known to be interpreted inconsistently, but I did not retrieve a source for this in this session.

## 5. Measuring whether an explanation was understood: retell or paraphrase tests, comprehension questions, self-explanation. Which are cheap enough for user testing?

### Takeaway
Self-explanation is both a strong learning intervention (meta-analytic g = 0.55) and a revealing measure: asking someone to explain or retell exposes gaps that self-rated understanding hides (IOED). For cheap user testing, the most diagnostic options are (1) a "rate your understanding, explain it, re-rate" IOED probe, (2) a free retell or paraphrase scored against the key idea units, and (3) two or three inference questions, not recall questions, because recall can be high even when the reader lacks deep understanding (McNamara et al.).

### Cited Findings
- Self-explanation meta-analysis: 64 reports, 69 effect sizes, random-effects g = 0.55. The effect held across most domains, ages and task types, and remained when researchers controlled for time on task — Bisra, Liu, Nesbit, Salimi & Winne (2018), *Educational Psychology Review*, [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/self-explanation-powerful-learning-technique-according-meta-analysis-64-studies); [PDF](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2018-bisra.pdf)
- Chi, de Leeuw, Chiu & LaVancher (1994): students prompted to self-explain each line of a text on the circulatory system gained more from pretest to posttest than controls who read it twice. All "high explainers" reached the correct mental model. Self-explanation protocols were used to diagnose mental models — [ASU record](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/eliciting-self-explanations-improves-understanding/); [PDF](https://pcl.sitehost.iu.edu/rgoldsto/courses/cogscilearning/chiselfexplanation.pdf)
- The IOED protocol (rate, explain, re-rate) reveals overconfidence in causal understanding. Mechanistic explanation prompts reduce the illusion, but listing reasons does not — [Rozenblit & Keil 2002](https://repositories.cdlib.org/content/qt582378mf/qt582378mf.pdf?t=op2kua); [Fernbach et al. 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23620547/)
- Recall and deep understanding can come apart. Coherent text raised everyone's recall, but deep (situation-model) understanding depended on prior knowledge × coherence — [McNamara et al. 1996, via BYU Editing Research](https://editingresearch.byu.edu/2022/11/23/how-less-clarity-could-improve-active-processing)
- NN/g measured mobile comprehension with comprehension tests on articles of set length and grade level, which shows a practical template for remote testing — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-content/)
- In the narrative meta-analysis, verbal tests showed a larger effect than written tests (g = .92 vs .49), so the test format itself shifts results — [Mar et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8219577/)

### Inferences
- A cheap protocol for 5–10 participants per explanation:
  1. Ask the reader to rate their understanding from 1 to 7.
  2. They read the explanation on their phone.
  3. Ask: "In a sentence or two, explain to a friend why …" (a mechanistic retell).
  4. Ask them to re-rate their understanding.
  5. Ask one or two inference questions that the text implies but does not state.

  Score the retell against 3–5 predefined idea units. A large drop in the re-rating, or missing idea units, signals a shallow or IOED-prone explanation.
- Use recall or multiple-choice questions only as a floor check. They inflate apparent understanding of smooth, explicit texts.
- Built into the product, a one-line "say it in your own words" prompt is also a learning intervention (g = 0.55), but it adds friction. Make it optional.
- Keep the test format the same when comparing versions, because format moves effect sizes.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve the Federal Plain Language Guidelines' section on paraphrase testing and controlled comparative studies, because the PDF could not be parsed. Their exact procedure and recommended sample sizes are therefore not cited here.
- I found no validated, cheap scoring rubric for adult retells of short explanations. Idea-unit scoring comes from the text-comprehension tradition and was not sourced in this session.

## 6. Narrative or story framing versus expository framing for comprehension and retention

### Takeaway
Narrative texts are understood and remembered better than expository texts. The meta-analysis covers more than 33,000 participants: overall g = 0.55, comprehension g = 0.48, memory g = 0.72, and adults g = 0.44. The authors warn against forcing all information into narrative form, and they found publication bias favouring narrative. Story elements must carry the core idea, otherwise they turn into seductive details.

### Cited Findings
- Mar, Li, Nguyen & Ta (2021, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*): 37 studies, 78 samples, 150 effect sizes, N = 33,078. Narrative beat expository with overall g = .55 [.31, .79]; comprehension g = .48 [.21, .75]; memory g = .72 [.43, 1.01]. Adults g = .44 and under-17s g = .67 (difference not significant). The effect was robust to controlling content and difficulty and to immediate versus delayed testing. The authors caution against forcing all information into narrative form, especially where that form is atypical, and note publication bias, especially in the memory studies — [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8219577/)
- Interesting but irrelevant detail hurts learning (g = -0.33), which is the main risk when stories are added as decoration rather than as the explanation itself — [Sundararajan & Adesope 2020, via ACU](https://inspire.acu.edu.au/articles/seductive-details)
- Explanatory (causal) knowledge is more prone to illusions of understanding than narrative knowledge, according to Rozenblit & Keil. Narratives feel understood, and the feeling is more often accurate, but they do not by themselves test causal understanding — [Wikipedia summary of Rozenblit & Keil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illusion_of_explanatory_depth)

### Inferences
- For scripture explanations: where the passage or its background is itself narrative (who, where, what happened, why it mattered to the first hearers), lead with that causal story. It is the content, not decoration, so it gains the narrative advantage without the seductive-detail penalty.
- For conceptual or linguistic points (a word's range of meaning, a grammatical construction), a short concrete scenario can help, but only if it carries the point. Otherwise use clear expository structure: claim, because, so.
- The adult effect (g ≈ 0.44 for comprehension and memory combined) is meaningful but modest. Narrative framing is a tool, not a rule.

### Gaps
- The Mar et al. meta-analysis compares whole narrative texts with whole expository texts. It does not test adding a narrative hook or frame to otherwise expository explanations, which is the more relevant design question.
- I found no studies on narrative versus expository framing for short (under 150 words) explanations read on phones.
