# Explain the passage plainly, then prove it

Yada's explanations become simple and deep together through one move. **The top layer makes one plain, causal claim about what is happening in the passage, and every layer beneath it adds evidence and nuance to that same claim**, without restating it in harder words or adding new topics. The learning science behind this is consistent. Cutting interesting but unneeded material, signalling structure and letting the reader pace the chunks have the strongest support. Narrative beats exposition for comprehension when the story is the content itself. Readers of different expertise need different amounts of support, which argues for two layers the reader chooses between, not graded rewrites. The best Bible explainers win the same way: BibleProject puts the storyline first, beginner commentaries land one point per passage, and the NET Bible labels every note by the kind of claim it makes. Each fails in a predictable direction: one tradition's emphases, devotional drift, sheer length, and Greek "fun facts" that commit the root fallacy. Disagreement needs the most care. Plain uncertainty statements keep trust in the source, but a bare "scholars disagree" is the one framing consistently linked to lower credibility. So Yada should name each reading, give its best evidence and its main difficulty in matched form, and say what everyone agrees on. On quality, accuracy should be a set of pass/fail gates checked claim by claim, mostly in code and by the adversary. John's limited attention should go to what models judge worst, which is fairness and whether the explanation actually helps. Testing should measure understanding through retells and inference questions, never through "was this helpful?" ratings. A concrete house style and review rubric close the report.

## Cutting, signalling and segmenting carry the most weight on a phone

The most useful evidence comes from Richard Mayer's multimedia learning programme, which carries over to short text read on a phone. In Mayer's own tallies, **coherence (excluding extraneous material) has a median effect of d = 0.86 and held in 18 of 19 tests; signalling the organisation of the essential ideas, d = 0.69 in 15 of 16; and learner-paced segmenting, d = 0.67 in 7 of 7** ([EdTech Books](https://edtechbooks.org/docx/10349)). These are upper bounds. An independent 2025 meta-analysis of 181 studies found an overall g = 0.37, with effects shrinking over time, and its largest effects included removing seductive detail and sentence-level coherence ([Cromley & Chen, NSF PAR](https://par.nsf.gov/search/author:%22Cromley,%20Jennifer%20G%22)). The harm from **seductive details** is the finding that matters most for Bible explainers: interesting but irrelevant material lowers learning by g = -0.33 across 58 studies, and by **g = -0.70 when the detail is tacked on at the end** ([ACU Inspire](https://inspire.acu.edu.au/articles/seductive-details); [WSU News](https://news.wsu.edu/2020/03/19/seductive-details-inhibit-learning/)). The colourful archaeology aside, the closing "fun fact" about a Greek word and the anecdote that warms up a point are the classic seductive details. They belong one tap down, if anywhere, never inline at the end of a guide.

Phones do not stop people understanding hard text, but they make it cost more. Across 1,629 reading instances, Nielsen Norman Group found **no practical comprehension difference between phone and desktop**, though phone readers slowed by about 30 ms per word on hard, roughly 990-word grade-12 articles. Easy articles in the same studies were about 400 words at grade 8 ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-content/)). The longer and denser a guide gets, the more effort it asks of a phone reader, so length matters most exactly where a passage is hardest.

Narrative is the second strong lever, with one condition attached. A meta-analysis of 33,078 participants found narrative texts beat expository ones, **g = 0.48 for comprehension and g = 0.72 for memory, with adults at g = 0.44**. The authors caution against forcing information into narrative form where it does not fit and note publication bias in the memory studies ([Mar et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8219577/)). For scripture, much of the narrative is already there. Who is speaking, to whom, what just happened and why it would have startled the first hearers is a causal story, and it is the explanation itself rather than decoration. That earns the narrative advantage without the seductive-detail penalty. For a grammatical or lexical point, the better structure is claim, because, so.

Plain-language standards then set the surface rules. ISO 24495-1:2023 defines plain language by outcome: readers get what they need, find it, understand it and can use it ([IPLF](https://www.iplfederation.org/the-iso-plain-language-standard-part1-and-part2)). Federal guidance keeps sentences under about 20 words, defines a necessary technical term on first use and spells out abbreviations ([Federal Plain Language Guidelines](https://revenue.ky.gov/DOR%20Training%20Materials/Federal%20Plain%20Language%20Guidelines.pdf)). GOV.UK writes to a **reading age of 9** ([Somerset Council, citing GDS](https://playbook.somerset.gov.uk/digital-inclusion-and-accessibility/creating-accessible-documents/document-language-and-reading-age)), and specialists prefer plain English too, more strongly as the issue gets more complex ([States of Jersey service manual](https://www.gov.je/ServiceManual/BrandDesign/Pages/TechnicalSpecialistContent.aspx)). Readability formulas only count word and sentence length. Raising a score does not usually raise comprehension, and formulas cannot see whether the content is right or well organised ([Government of Canada, Our Languages](https://www.noslangues-ourlanguages.gc.ca/en/blogue-blog/readability-formulas-eng); [Effortmark](https://effortmark.co.uk/?p=3586)). Yada should treat a grade score as a lint warning, not a target, for a reason covered below: pushing the grade level down is what makes models invent things.

One term needs care. Plain language does not mean dropping the words the Bible's argument depends on. Reviewing N. T. Wright's *For Everyone* series, Modern Reformation's reviewer argued that Wright's fresh translation hid terms such as "covenant" that the glossary then had to define ([Modern Reformation](https://www.modernreformation.org/resources/articles/mark-for-everyone-and-luke-for-everyone-by-n-t-wright)). If a reader sees "righteousness" in the BSB and the guide says only "being in the right", the reader cannot connect the two. Yada should keep the load-bearing term the reader sees on the page and define it once, in plain words.

## Two layers beat graded rewrites, and a smooth top layer can fake understanding

Layering is supported, though not in the "explain like I'm five, then fifteen, then expert" form. The **expertise reversal effect** shows that support which helps novices often does nothing for, or harms, readers with more knowledge, who are slowed by guidance they don't need ([Kalyuga 2007](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007%2Fs10648-007-9054-3)). McNamara and Kintsch found that a fully explicit text raised recall for everyone. Yet high-knowledge readers built deeper, inferential understanding from a less explicit version that made them fill gaps, while low-knowledge readers needed the explicit one ([BYU Editing Research](https://editingresearch.byu.edu/2022/11/23/how-less-clarity-could-improve-active-processing)). One fixed level cannot serve both groups, so the reader should choose the depth. Usability research sets the limit: progressive disclosure helps, but **designs with more than two disclosure levels typically fail because users get lost**, and each step down needs strong information scent ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progressive-disclosure/)). Yada's existing Read → Understand → Evidence structure fits this. Read is the text, Understand is the plain layer, and Evidence is the deep layer, so there are two explanation layers under the text. Nothing in the evidence supports adding a third rewrite level.

The risk lies in the top layer. Rozenblit and Keil showed that people rate their understanding of causal systems highly, then mark it down sharply once asked to explain the mechanism step by step. **The illusion is strongest for explanatory knowledge and weaker for facts and narratives** ([Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illusion_of_explanatory_depth); [original](https://repositories.cdlib.org/content/qt582378mf/qt582378mf.pdf?t=op2kua)). Asking for a mechanistic explanation shrank the illusion, while asking people to list reasons did not ([Fernbach et al. 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23620547/)). A fluent two-sentence summary can therefore leave a reader feeling they understand Romans 9 without understanding it. The defence is to make the top layer causal, saying why something happens and not just what, with explicit "because" and "so". It should also say in the same layer what remains open, so that the deep layer never reveals a doubt the top layer hid. Instructional-explanation research points the same way. Explanations often fail because readers stay passive and the explanation does not target what they are likely to get wrong ([Wittwer & Renkl summary](https://theelearningcoach.com/elearning_design/instructional-explanations/)). Depth, for a layperson, means naming the likely misreading and resolving it from the text, not adding more facts.

## The best explainers win by orientation and lose in predictable directions

The explainers lay readers credit share a few habits. BibleProject builds each short unit, around five minutes, on one big idea. It places a passage inside a single storyline that leads to Jesus, and it teaches readers to notice repeated words and parallel scenes for themselves ([BibleProject: Design patterns](https://bibleproject.com/explore/video/design-patterns-biblical-narrative/)). The short video is distilled from hours of podcast conversation in which a lay questioner prompts a scholar, a format reviewers describe as easy to follow even on hard issues ([Christian Courier](https://www.christiancourier.ca/making-better-bible-readers/)). The earlier pain-point research found the same thing from the other side: orientation, meaning where a passage sits in the whole story and who wrote to whom, is the most cited reason the Bible "clicks" for beginners ([Journey Chattanooga](https://journeychattanooga.com/bible/)). Beginner commentaries add **one passage, one main point, with technical detail subordinated to it**. Wright's series assumes no jargon, sets each passage in its political and social world and keeps the reader on the main point ([Accordance](https://www.accordancebible.com/product/for-everyone-the-new-testament-commentary-n-t-wright-18-volumes/)). The Bible Speaks Today aims to expound accurately, relate the text to life and stay readable ([IVP](https://ivpress.com/the-bible-speaks-today-series)). Fee and Stuart supply the control rule: establish what a text meant "then and there" before asking what it means now, because a text cannot mean what it never could have meant to its first hearers. They also reject the idea that meaning is plain to any sincere reader ([Logos](https://www.logos.com/product/5421); [Harper review](https://barbaraleeharper.com/2017/03/16/book-review-how-to-read-the-bible-for-all-its-worth/)).

The NET Bible offers the convention that maps most directly onto Yada. Its 60,000+ notes are typed: **tn** for translation choices and interpretive options, **sn** for background and theology, **tc** for manuscript variants ([Bible Gateway](https://biblegateway.com/versions/New-English-Translation-NET-Bible)). The stated aim is that an English reader "knows at a glance what the alternatives are" ([Andy Naselli](https://andynaselli.com/the-net-bible)). Yada's claim categories (text, context, interpretation, debated) already do this job. The research suggests keeping them strictly separate in the prose too, so a reader can always tell what the text shows from what someone infers. The NIV Cultural Backgrounds Study Bible shows the value of a separate background layer. Walton and Keener's notes explain why a verse would have surprised its first hearers without telling the reader what to conclude ([Accordance](https://www.accordancebible.com/product/niv-cultural-backgrounds-study-bible-notes/)).

Each format fails in a direction its design invites. BibleProject's critics are almost all Reformed. Their objection is to what it emphasises or leaves out, such as penal substitution, wrath and hell, not to factual errors, and even critics praise its quality ([Servants of Grace](https://servantsofgrace.org/the-bible-project-theological-drift/); [Christian Courier](https://www.christiancourier.ca/making-better-bible-readers/); [ACL / TGC Australia](https://acl.asn.au/review-the-bible-project-brilliant-but-flawed/)). Wright's series is faulted for New Perspective framing that "faintly but surely misleads" in its reviewer's view ([Modern Reformation](https://www.modernreformation.org/resources/articles/mark-for-everyone-and-luke-for-everyone-by-n-t-wright)). The Bible Knowledge Commentary is openly dispensational, though it is still credited with presenting other views fairly ([Best Bible Commentaries](https://www.bestbiblecommentaries.com/bible-knowledge-commentary/)). The pattern is that every single voice carries a tradition, and the fairest ones declare it and still show the alternatives. Application-led formats such as the Life Application Study Bible, where over 75% of features are application ([Conversant Faith](https://conversantfaith.com/2019/11/08/life-application-study-bible-review/)), draw the warning that notes can be wrong and tempt readers to read notes instead of the text ([Verse by Verse Ministry](https://versebyverseministry.org/bible-answers/is-the-life-application-study-bible-appropriate-for-serious-study-of-the-bi)). Comprehensive study Bibles fail on length by design. The ESV Study Bible sells 1.1 million words of notes as a feature ([Crossway](https://www.crossway.org/bibles/esv-study-bible-personal-size-tpb/)). Opening anecdotes, Wright's signature, add warmth but also length, and they bring in the writer's own world. For an in-app guide, historical setting makes a passage vivid more honestly.

The worst failure belongs to the word study. D. A. Carson's root fallacy is the error of reading a word's meaning from its parts or etymology. His standard example is the claim that *dynamis* in Romans 1:16 means "dynamite", which he calls reverse etymology compounded by anachronism ([Wisconsin Lutheran Seminary review](https://www.wisluthsem.org/review-exegetical-fallacies/)). Teachers add "meaning soup", piling every listed sense into one occurrence, and the principle that words mean what they were used to mean in their period ([Logos, Mark Ward](https://www.logos.com/grow/better-resources-than-strongs-concordance/); [ChurchLeaders](https://churchleaders.com/pastors/pastor-articles/166406-nicholas-mcdonald-7-ways-bad-word-study.html)). This is why original-language notes are evidence and not the point. A Greek or Hebrew word earns a place on screen only when its contextual sense in this verse, taken from the source data, changes or clarifies what the English says.

One risk stays a hypothesis. Pattern-finding of the BibleProject kind, such as chiasms and design links across books, could over-read the text, but no retrieved source documents a specific error. Yada should treat a literary-structure claim as interpretation unless the repetition is visible in the supplied text.

## "Scholars disagree" is the one uncertainty framing that costs trust

The research on uncertainty is more reassuring than writers usually assume. In five UK experiments with 5,780 people, stating uncertainty about a figure lowered confidence in the figure but **did not reduce trust in the source**, and reactions did not differ by political ideology ([van der Bles et al. 2020, Groningen](https://research.rug.nl/en/publications/the-effects-of-communicating-uncertainty-on-public-trust-in-facts/)). A review of 48 experiments found one consistent exception. **Consensus uncertainty, meaning disagreement among experts, produced most of the negative effects on credibility and was never found to help** ([Gustafson & Rice 2020](https://rrice.faculty.comm.ucsb.edu/A149GustafsonRice2020ReviewEffectsUncertaintyPublicScienceCommunication.pdf)). In a pre-registered study of AI answers to medical questions, natural-language uncertainty reduced overreliance, and the exact wording mattered enough that the authors urge testing it before deployment ([Kim et al., FAccT 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.00623)). For Yada, a specific statement such as "the Greek word can mean X or Y, and the next verse favours X" keeps trust. A bare "this is debated" signals that experts are fighting without showing why, which is the version linked to lower credibility. This research is about numbers and science, not contested readings of texts, so the transfer is an inference, but it points in a clear direction: disagreement should be shown through its evidence, not announced.

Readers and pastors want contested passages addressed rather than avoided. **Only 5% of pastors think AI should avoid contested scripture**, even though 94% worry it will misinterpret it ([Business Wire](https://secure.businesswire.com/news/home/20260826106650/en/Only-5-of-Pastors-Think-AI-Should-Avoid-Contested-Scripture-Despite-Widespread-Concern-About-Misinterpretation)). The documented default for Bible-app chatbots is a lean toward US conservative evangelical readings on questions where churches differ, which the Bible Society attributes to the makeup of the English-language web ([Scripture Engagement](https://scripture-engagement.org/content/ai-bible-apps-and-theological-bias/); [FSSPX News](https://fsspx.news/fr/news/bible-et-ia-le-biais-evangelique-americain-57051)). Yada's generator will drift that way unless the format prevents it.

The fairest published model is Vern Poythress's survey of ten evangelical views of Genesis 1–2. Each view gets a description, its supporting logic and a labelled criticisms section. He says every view has its own challenges and closes by urging caution beyond what the text says ([TGC](https://www.thegospelcoalition.org/essay/evangelical-interpretations-genesis-1-2/)). The treatment is the same for every view, and that sameness is what makes it fair. 1 Corinthians 14:34–35 shows the second move, NET's separation of manuscript questions from interpretive ones. The verses sit after verse 33 in some manuscripts and after verse 40 in others. That is a fact readers of every tradition can accept, and it is distinct from the readings built on it: a later scribal addition, Paul quoting the Corinthians and rebutting them in verse 36, a ban on women judging prophecy, and the traditional general prohibition ([Marg Mowczko](https://margmowczko.com/pauls-theology-1-corinthians-14-34-35/); [Scarletta](https://scarletta.substack.com/p/6-interpretations-of-1-corinthians)). Stating the agreed fact first gives every reader common ground before the views divide.

Four rules follow, and the last two are inferences that need testing with readers. First, name each view by what it says about the text ("the framework view", "the quotation reading"), not by a camp label ("egalitarian", "complementarian") or a loaded one ("the non-literal view"). The tradition that holds a view is useful information, but it belongs in the view's description, attributed. Second, give each view the same parts: what it says, what supporters point to, its main difficulty. Third, keep the views close in length, since uneven length is the easiest bias to spot. Fourth, keep "clearly", "obviously" and "most scholars" out unless a source supports the majority claim. Yada's pipeline already forbids a winner field and a silent winner by ordering or hedging, and the research supports that rule. The remaining risk is that a reader infers a winner from which view comes first. Yada should order views by one declared rule across the corpus, such as oldest attested reading first, rather than case by case.

## Accuracy is a gate, judgment is a scale, and John's attention is the scarce resource

The AI evaluation literature agrees that long-form explanations fail on support, not fluency. FActScore breaks text into atomic facts and found ChatGPT biographies only **58% supported** ([FActScore](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.14251)). Even the best systems in the ALCE benchmark **lacked full citation support about half the time** ([ALCE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14627v2)). The scripture-specific failure is misquotation. YouVersion's CEO says the best models misquote scripture at least **15% of the time and some as much as 60%**, counting any change to a word or punctuation mark, and that users often cannot spot altered verses ([Christian Post](https://www.christianpost.com/news/ais-bible-misquotes-range-from-15-to-60-youversion-ceo.html)). His method is unpublished, so the figure is indicative only. The stakes are high because trust runs ahead of accuracy: nearly **1 in 3 US adults rate AI spiritual advice as trustworthy as a pastor's** ([Gloo](https://gloo.com/press/releases/ai-is-becoming-a-spiritual-authority-in-americans-lives-new-research-reveals)).

Simplifying makes the problem worse. In FactPICO's 345 plain-language summaries of medical trials, **the simpler the output, the less factual it was**. Models that simplified by rephrasing hallucinated significantly more and dropped important information, while the most extractive model was the most accurate but read at an advanced level ([FactPICO](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.11456)). This is the central risk for Yada's goal, and it shapes the pipeline design. Generate the accurate claim set first from the supplied source data. Simplify the wording as a separate step that must keep the same claims. Have the adversary check what went missing as well as what was added. Yada's adversary verdicts (upheld, unsupported, miscategorised, compound, overclaimed) cover additions well. They have no verdict for "lost a qualifier" or "dropped the agreed fact", and the FactPICO evidence says that is where simplification fails.

How the adversary checks matters as much as having one. Models asked to critique their own reasoning without new evidence do not reliably improve and sometimes get worse. Much of the apparent gain from multi-agent debate comes from aggregating several generations ([Huang et al., ICLR 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.01798v2)). Chain-of-Verification works better: the verifier answers its own questions without seeing the draft, so the draft cannot bias the answers ([Dhuliawala et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.11495)). Adversarial debate does help a non-expert judge reach the truth, raising human-judge accuracy from 60% to 88% on a reading-comprehension benchmark ([Khan et al., ICML 2024](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/khan24a.html)). That is a good model for the adversary arguing a claim in front of John. Model judges agree with humans over 80% of the time, about as often as humans agree with each other, but they favour longer answers, answer position and their own outputs ([Zheng et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.05685v4)). Stated confidence runs high, and consistency across several samples is a better signal ([Xiong et al., ICLR 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13063v2)). In practice the adversary should come from a different model family or at least a different role and prompt. It should see the source data but not the generator's reasoning. A claim that shows up in only one of several sampled generations should be flagged as uncertain.

That leaves the question of what John should look at. The AIS protocol judges each statement with two yes/no questions: is it interpretable, and would a rater accept "according to [source], [statement]"? Because interpretability comes first, an unclear claim fails before its sourcing is judged ([cross-lingual attribution paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.14332)). Binary judgements on atomic claims are faster and more reliable than Likert scores, and they stop a high average hiding one invented claim. Exact quote matches, valid references, Strong's numbers and parses taken from the data, and the ban on licensed text are mechanical checks that code should do. The adversary, with John looking at its flags, should judge attribution. John's own judgement is needed for fairness, plainness and whether the guide addresses the right misreading, which models judge worst. Shankar's EvalGen work adds two practical points. Reviewers' criteria change as they grade ("criteria drift"), so the rubric must be a living, versioned document. And automated checks should be kept or dropped according to how well they agree with the human's grades ([Shankar et al., UIST 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.12272)). Yada already logs every decision to `review/decisions.jsonl`, which is the raw material for measuring adversary–John agreement by verdict type.

A single reviewer cannot measure agreement between reviewers, but he can measure his own consistency. He can re-review a blind 5–10% sample after a gap, seed known-bad "canary" entries to check his attention, and occasionally ask a reviewer from a different tradition to grade a small sample. That last check measures bias in the reviewer, not only in the model. These are standard practices, not sourced findings, and no source quantified safe batch sizes for review fatigue.

## Test understanding with retells, not ratings

"Was this helpful?" measures the feeling of understanding, which is the thing the illusion of explanatory depth inflates. Better measures already exist. Self-explanation is both a strong learning intervention, **g = 0.55 across 64 reports**, holding when time on task is controlled ([Bisra et al. 2018](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/self-explanation-powerful-learning-technique-according-meta-analysis-64-studies)), and a diagnostic. Chi's students who explained a text line by line exposed their mental models, and all the "high explainers" reached the correct one ([Chi et al. 1994](https://pcl.sitehost.iu.edu/rgoldsto/courses/cogscilearning/chiselfexplanation.pdf)). The rate, explain, re-rate procedure shows whether a confident reader can actually explain ([Fernbach et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23620547/)). Recall and deep understanding can come apart: explicit text raises recall for everyone while inferential understanding depends on prior knowledge ([BYU Editing Research](https://editingresearch.byu.edu/2022/11/23/how-less-clarity-could-improve-active-processing)). So multiple-choice recall should only be a floor check. Format also shifts results, with verbal tests showing larger effects than written ones in the narrative meta-analysis ([Mar et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8219577/)). When two versions are compared, the test format must stay the same.

A protocol cheap enough for one founder looks like this. Recruit five to ten lay readers per test passage, from at least three traditions. On their own phones, they rate their understanding of the passage from 1 to 7 after reading only the text. Then they read the guide and explain to a friend, in a sentence or two, why the passage says what it says. They re-rate their understanding and answer two inference questions the guide implies but does not state. One multiple-choice item, written from the passage and not the guide, includes a distractor that matches the common misreading. The retell is scored against three to five idea units John writes before the session. A guide is shallow if readers' confidence rises while their retells miss the causal idea, or if many still pick the misreading distractor. A delayed retell a few days later separates real understanding from fluency. For disagreement, a blind test works best: show a reader the "where interpretation differs" section and ask which tradition they think wrote it. If readers of every tradition cannot tell, the parity rules are working. The sample sizes and this fairness probe are inferences, since no published study has tested AI Bible explanations with lay readers, and no validated instrument for perceived fairness across traditions was found.

Trust calibration matters more than trust. Users should rely on the guide more when it is right than when it is wrong, and the medical-AI work shows that uncertainty wording moves reliance ([Kim et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.00623)). Testing that needs deliberately weakened explanations. Those belong only in moderated sessions with a debrief, never in a shipped bundle, consistent with Yada's rule that only John-approved content ships.

## Yada house style for explanations

The style below turns the evidence into rules. It uses the existing data shape (`summary_one_sentence`, `key_ideas`, `movement`, `whats_happening`, claims grouped as "What we can see" and "Where interpretation differs", positions and evidence). Where a number is a judgement rather than a measured threshold, the table says so.

### Layers and lengths

| Layer | What it answers | Length | Evidence basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Read** | The text itself; nothing added | n/a | Notes displacing the text are a known failure |
| **Passage orientation** (`summary_one_sentence`) | Where this passage sits: who is speaking, to whom, what just happened | One sentence, at most 25 words | Orientation is what makes beginners "click"; storyline first |
| **How the passage moves** (`movement`) | The steps of the argument or story | 2–5 steps, each label at most 10 words, phrased as what happens | Signalling; segmenting |
| **What's happening** (`whats_happening`) | The 30-second causal answer: what is going on and why it matters here | 2–4 sentences, about 40–80 words, with at least one "because" or "so" | Coherence; IOED needs mechanism, not summary |
| **What we can see** | Text and context claims, ranked for orientation | Up to 3 on the first screen, one sentence each, at most 25 words | Two-level disclosure; existing 3/2 cap |
| **Where interpretation differs** | Interpretation and debated claims | Up to 2 on the first screen; debated claims open into positions | Disagreement shown, not announced |
| **Evidence** ("Why do we say that?") | The source for one claim: verse, morphology, lexicon, apparatus, or "general knowledge" when model knowledge | 1–3 sentences plus the cited evidence | AIS attribution; NET typing |
| **Word note** | What this word means in this verse, if that changes the reading | One sentence of contextual sense, gloss from the data | Root fallacy; "meaning soup" |

A passage's first screen of explanation, before any tap, should stay under about 150 words. That is a judgement drawn from NN/g's mobile findings and the coherence principle, not a tested threshold, and the user tests above should adjust it. A passage of more than about 20 verses should get more `movement` steps, not a longer `whats_happening`.

### Wording rules

| Rule | Do | Don't |
|---|---|---|
| Sentences | Average 15–20 words; none over 25 | Stack clauses to fit a qualifier in |
| Reading level | Aim for a reading age of about 9–12 on the top layer; treat the score as a warning | Rephrase a claim to lower the score if that changes what it says |
| Load-bearing terms | Keep the word the reader sees in their translation ("covenant", "righteousness") and define it once in plain words | Swap it for a paraphrase the reader can't trace back to the page |
| Original-language words | Say what the word means *in this verse*, using the supplied gloss, and why that matters | Give etymology, an English cognate ("dynamite"), every sense at once, or "literally means" |
| Fact vs inference | State the fact, then the inference, in separate sentences and claims | "Paul is clearly correcting disruptive women" |
| Uncertainty | Hedge once, at the claim, with its basis: "The word can mean X or Y; verse 36 favours Y" | Hedge every sentence, or write a bare "scholars disagree" |
| Consistency across layers | The top layer mentions that a question is open when the deep layer shows it is | A confident top layer with doubt revealed only underneath |
| Asides | Background only when it explains why the passage would surprise its first hearers | Trivia, anecdotes, or a closing "fun fact" |
| Application | None. Say what it meant then; leave what to do now to the reader | "This means you should…", even framed as context |
| Voice | Plain, warm, unhurried, third person about the text | Counselling, preaching, "we believe", exclamation, AI-assistant phrasing |
| Labels | Category labels only for exceptions, as the design brief already sets | Restating the category in the prose ("it is disputed that…") |

### How disagreement is presented

A debated claim follows the same shape everywhere. It opens with **the question in plain words**, framed as what the text leaves open rather than as a fight. Then comes **what is agreed**, the shared facts every position accepts: the wording, the manuscript placement, the grammar. Then **the positions, each with a neutral name** describing its reading of the text, in a fixed order set by one corpus-wide rule (for example, oldest attested reading first). Each position has the same three parts: *what it says*, *what supporters point to* (with evidence) and *its main difficulty*. Positions should be within about 20% of each other in word count, which is a parity heuristic, not a sourced figure. A tradition is named only as an attribution inside a position ("held by many Reformed and Catholic interpreters"), never as the position's name, and only where the attribution is sourced. Manuscript questions are kept apart from interpretive questions, the NET tc/tn split, and stated first because they are facts. There is no winner field and no simulated winner, no "most scholars" without a source, and no "clearly" or "obviously". A position's main difficulty must be one its own supporters would recognise as the real difficulty.

### Review rubric

Gates are pass/fail, and any failure blocks approval whatever the scores. Scales are scored 1–5 against written anchors. Because the review tool already refuses approval below 4, the entry's rating should be its **lowest** scale score, not the average, so a fair but impenetrable entry cannot pass on its other strengths.

The rubric should change under three disciplines. Every change is versioned in the decision log, with the anchors re-read whenever John notices his standard shifting. A blind 5–10% sample of past approvals is re-graded when anchors change. And adversary–John agreement is tracked per verdict type, with the adversary prompt reworked for any category where agreement is low.

## Conclusion

The research changes where the difficulty lies. Simplicity and depth are not really in tension for Yada's readers. They conflict only when simplifying means rephrasing a claim until it says something slightly different, which is what models do when pushed toward a lower grade level. The fix is to separate the two jobs. The model and the adversary fix the claim set against the source data first. Wording is simplified second, under a rule that no claim or qualifier may change. That puts the hard accuracy work in code and the adversary, and leaves John the work only a person can do: deciding whether a guide is fair to readers who disagree and whether it addresses the misreading a newcomer is actually likely to make.

The second shift concerns what "where Christians read it differently" is for. The evidence suggests it is less a scholarly courtesy than the main trust mechanism. Announced disagreement lowers credibility, shown disagreement with agreed facts first keeps it, and chatbots and single-publisher notes both fall short here in documented ways. Yada's untested assumptions sit in exactly two places: whether readers of every tradition see its parity rules as fair, and whether a 150-word first screen produces real retells rather than the feeling of understanding. Both can be tested with ten readers and a retell prompt before the passage-level corpus scales.
